# Academic Sources: Prior Work and Citation

Entering the scholarly conversation, representing others fairly, and attaching citations honestly.

## 1. Enter a scholarly conversation

Academic arguments respond to other arguments. Pattern: existing position, limitation or tension or open question, new claim.

Do not write literature reviews that read "Smith did X. Jones did Y. Lee did Z." Synthesize the structure of the field:

> Existing approaches fall into three groups: static dependency recovery, runtime tracing, and configuration-based inference. Static approaches offer broad coverage but approximate dynamic dispatch. Tracing identifies precise observed edges but depends on workload coverage. Configuration analysis exposes possible connectivity but not invocation.

Organize literature by position, assumptions, methods, findings, disagreement, limitations, or conceptual categories. The reader should learn the shape of the literature, not its bibliography.

## 2. Represent prior work fairly

State another scholar's position in a form its proponents would recognize.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| Prior work fails to find all dependencies. | Prior work optimizes observed runtime precision and therefore does not attempt to recover unexecuted paths. |

Distinguish what a source states, what it demonstrates, what you infer, and what you argue beyond it. Do not attribute your inference to the source.

## 3. Make novelty relative

Avoid universal novelty claims unless the evidence supports them.

| Risky | Better |
|---|---|
| No one has studied X. | We are not aware of prior work that combines X and Y for Z. |
| | Existing approaches recover C but do not model D explicitly. |
| | Prior studies examine X primarily in A. Its implications for B remain less well understood. |

Precision creates credibility.

## 4. Cite claims, not paragraphs

Place citations as close as possible to the proposition they support. The reader should not infer what a citation establishes.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| ReAct interleaves reasoning and actions. It influenced later systems. Tool use became common. [citation] | ReAct interleaves reasoning traces with actions [citation]. (Then cite other propositions separately.) |

## 5. Choose sources for the claim being made

- original papers for who introduced a method or result
- systematic reviews or surveys for field-wide conclusions
- canonical works for established theories
- standards for normative requirements
- official sources for official measurements
- primary documents for claims about those documents

A famous source is not automatically the right source. Do not cite a single primary study as evidence of field-wide consensus.

## 6. Never use citations decoratively

Before attaching a citation ask: Does the source support this proposition? At this level of certainty? For this population or setting? If not, narrow the claim or use another source.

## 7. Separate source, interpretation, and contribution

The reader must be able to distinguish their idea, their evidence, your interpretation, your extension, your result.

> Smith argues X. This explanation assumes Y. In our setting, Y does not hold because Z. We therefore model Q instead.

Do not let your contribution disappear inside cited prose.

## 8. Paraphrase ideas, not syntax

Do not paraphrase by swapping synonyms into the source sentence. Read, understand, reconstruct the idea, verify accuracy, cite. Quote only when the exact language matters.

## 9. Quote sparingly

Quote when analyzing language, preserving an authoritative definition, the wording is historically significant, or paraphrase would erase a meaningful distinction.

Do not outsource reasoning to a quotation. Explain why the quotation matters.
