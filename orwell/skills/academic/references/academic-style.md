# Academic Style and Structure

Paragraphs, cohesion, emphasis, terminology, headings, formality, and venue.

## 1. Give each paragraph one controlling purpose

A strong paragraph makes one argumentative move: point, development, evidence, interpretation, connection.

The first sentence establishes the paragraph's purpose. The final sentence leaves the argument farther ahead than where it began. Split paragraphs when the controlling claim changes.

## 2. Build cohesion from old information to new

Start with information already active in the discourse and move toward new information.

> The analyzer constructs a call graph from these callsites. This graph is then augmented with deployment metadata.

The new information from one sentence becomes the starting point of the next.

## 3. Put important information in emphatic positions

Readers notice paragraph openings, grammatical subjects, sentence endings, and paragraph endings.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| A 37% reduction in false positives was produced by provenance filtering. | Provenance filtering reduced false positives by 37%. |

## 4. Use metadiscourse only when it helps navigation

| Useful | Weak |
|---|---|
| We first define the threat model and then evaluate recovery under three workload conditions. | In this section, we will talk about several issues related to... |
| This distinction matters for two reasons. | |

Signpost the argument. Do not narrate obvious acts of writing.

## 5. Use transitions to name real logical relations

Transitions signal addition, contrast, consequence, qualification, example, restatement, or sequence. Do not insert them mechanically. "However" cannot create a contrast that does not exist.

## 6. Define terms when their boundaries matter

Define a term when readers may interpret it differently, it has a technical meaning, competing definitions exist, or the argument depends on its boundary.

Do not define ordinary words to appear rigorous. After defining a term, use it consistently.

## 7. Introduce abbreviations only when they save work

Define at first meaningful use: "system dependency graph (SDG)". Then use consistently. Do not abbreviate terms used only a few times. Avoid several visually similar abbreviations.

## 8. Use headings as an argument map

When venue conventions permit, headings expose the logic of the argument.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| Background | Static topology underdetermines runtime dependencies |
| More Results | Callsite analysis recovers candidate service edges |
| Other Issues | Runtime evidence resolves ambiguous targets |

Standard headings such as Methods and Results may be required. Follow venue rules. Do not over-section: a heading marks a meaningful conceptual unit.

## 9. Keep parallel ideas grammatically parallel

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| The system aims to recover callsites, resolving targets, and provenance tracking. | The system aims to recover callsites, resolve targets, and track provenance. |

Use parallel structure for contributions, research questions, hypotheses, lists, and section summaries.

## 10. Use lists only for parallel material

Do not turn complex prose into bullets because it is complex. Use a list in four cases. The number of items matters, the items have comparable status, readers need to refer back to them, or the list exposes structure better than prose. Introduce the category that governs the list.

## 11. Control sentence complexity

Complex ideas may require complex sentences. The rule is not "write only short sentences." The rule is: **make the grammatical structure simpler than the intellectual structure.** One sentence carries one principal argumentative action. Vary length deliberately.

## 12. Avoid false formality

Academic prose does not require archaic words, passive voice everywhere, inflated abstractions, long sentences, excessive nominalization, Latin phrases, or avoidance of first person.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| An evaluation of three systems is undertaken herein. | We evaluate three systems. |

Use first person when it clarifies responsibility: We define, We argue, We measured. Follow venue conventions when they differ.

## 13. Do not confuse complexity with rigor

Rigor comes from explicit assumptions, defensible reasoning, appropriate evidence, reproducible methods, precise definitions, and careful qualification. Not from difficult prose. When two formulations are equally accurate, prefer the easier one.

## 14. Treat reader attention as a scarce resource

Every unexplained abbreviation, ambiguous pronoun, delayed definition, unnecessary qualification, and digression consumes attention. Spend it where the intellectual problem requires it. Complex subject matter may be unavoidable. Unnecessarily complex exposition is not.

## 15. Respect genre and venue

Explicit requirements outrank generic style advice. Follow, in order: conference or journal instructions, disciplinary conventions, assignment requirements, required citation style, publisher house style.

Do not "improve" prose into noncompliance. When Chicago or Turabian is required, use the requested system consistently and follow the applicable edition rather than remembered formatting.

## 16. Keep necessary reasoning out of footnotes

Footnotes may hold citations, bounded qualifications, useful but nonessential detail, and historiographic context. If the reader needs a point to accept the central claim, put it in the main text.
